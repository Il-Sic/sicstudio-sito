Myth

The Argonauts in brief

In Greek myth the Argonauts are the heroes who set off with Jason on the ship Argo to bring back the golden fleece from Colchis, at the far end of the Black Sea. King Pelias had imposed the voyage on Jason, hoping he would not come back.

Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.1–17

The fullest version that has come down to us is a Greek poem of the third century BC in four books, the Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes. But the ship was famous long before: Homer already calls it “Argo, known to all”.

Homer, Odyssey 12.69–72

Myth

The ship that spoke

It was built by Argus, son of Arestor, together with Athena and following her advice.

Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.18–19 and 1.111–114

Into the keel, at the bow, Athena had fitted a beam from the sacred oak of Dodona, the sanctuary of Zeus. That's why, on the day of departure, the ship itself cried out, eager to put to sea.

Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.524–527; Apollodorus, Library 1.9.16

On the way home the beam spoke with a human voice: the heroes would not escape the storms until Circe had purified them.

Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4.580–592; Apollodorus, Library 1.9.24

And the name? Some say it comes from its builder, others from the Greek word argós, “swift”.

Apollodorus, Library 1.9.16; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 4.41.3

Why the app's guide is called Argo

In Argonauta, Argo is your ship, which grows with your routes, and it's also who answers your questions. Like the ship of the myth, which carried a beam that could speak. But Argo answers only with what's in the cards of the Atlas, and shows you which card the answer comes from.

Myth

The heroes aboard

In the app, these five come aboard your Argo as you explore. In the poem they are among the fifty or more heroes Apollonius lists at the start of the first book.

  • Orpheus the singer

    His song charmed rocks and rivers. To the sound of his lyre the heroes beat their oars.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.23–34 and 1.536–541

  • Tiphys the helmsman

    He read the sea, the winds, the sun and the stars. The heroes gave him the helm.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.105–110 and 1.400–401

  • Mopsus the seer

    Apollo had taught him the omens of birds. A kingfisher told him the storm was over.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.65–66 and 1.1084–1087

  • Lynceus the sharp-eyed

    He had the sharpest eyes of all: it was said he could see even under the earth.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.151–155

  • Heracles the strongest

    He rowed so hard that he broke his oar. Searching for young Hylas he stayed ashore, and the ship sailed on without him.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.1153–1171 and 1.1207–1283

Myth

The voyage to the golden fleece

In the app every route you complete brings Argo to the next landfall. They are six moments of Apollonius' voyage, from Pagasae to the golden fleece.

  1. 1 Pagasae

    The heroes push Argo into the sea from the harbour of Pagasae and set off at dawn, to the sound of Orpheus' lyre. The centaur Chiron comes down from Pelion to the shore to see them off.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.234–579

  2. 2 Lemnos

    Only women lived on the island: the year before they had killed their men. Queen Hypsipyle welcomes the heroes, and the departure is put off day after day, until Heracles calls them back to the voyage.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.607–909

  3. 3 Cyzicus

    King Cyzicus welcomes them as friends. After they leave, a storm drives them back at night, and they fight without recognising each other. After the mourning, Mopsus understands from the cry of a kingfisher that they must honour Rhea on Mount Dindymum.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.936–1152

  4. 4 The Clashing Rocks

    At the entrance to the Black Sea two rocks crash together. Following the advice of old Phineus, the heroes release a dove and row with all their strength; Athena pushes the ship with her hand, and from then on the rocks stand still. Apollonius calls them the Cyanean Rocks.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 2.317–340 and 2.549–618

  5. 5 Colchis

    They sail up the river Phasis to the city of Aea. King Aeetes promises Jason the fleece if he yokes two fire-breathing bulls; Medea, the king's daughter, gives him an ointment that protects him for one day.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 2.1260–1285; 3.401–421 and 3.1042–1051

  6. 6 The Golden Fleece

    The fleece hangs from an oak in the sacred grove of Ares, guarded by a serpent that never sleeps. Medea lulls it to sleep with her song and a potion; Jason takes the fleece from the tree, and at dawn they set sail again.

    Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4.109–211

In the sky

Argo among the stars

At the end of the voyage the ship rises to the sky: Valerius Flaccus has it settle among the stars.

Valerius Flaccus, Argonautica 1.1–4

Aratus sees it beside the tail of the Great Dog, “stern foremost”, like the ships that sailors turn round to enter harbour. From the prow to the mast it is dark; the rest shines.

Aratus, Phaenomena 342–352

Since 1756, when Lacaille divided it, the ship in the sky has been three constellations: Carina, the keel; Puppis, the stern; and Vela, the sails. Its brightest star is Canopus, on the steering oar.

I. Ridpath, Star Tales; International Astronomical Union

A curiosity

The argonaut is also an octopus

In the Mediterranean lives an octopus that bears the name of Argo's sailors: Argonauta argo. The female builds a shell as thin as paper, which isn't a true shell: it's the cradle of her eggs.

FAO, Cephalopods of the World, vol. 3, 2016, pp. 228–231

Aristotle called it “the sailor” and imagined it going to sea with a sail. Today we know the shell is for the eggs and for floating.

Aristotle, History of Animals 9.37
